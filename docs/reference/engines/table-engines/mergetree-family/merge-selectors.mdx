Insert operations into `MergeTree`-family tables create a new data part each time.
A background process periodically merges existing parts into bigger ones, which
bounds the number of parts a query has to open and removes data made obsolete by
engines such as `ReplacingMergeTree`.

The [`merge_selector_algorithm`](/reference/settings/merge-tree-settings/merge-selector#merge_selector_algorithm)
table setting chooses the algorithm that picks which parts to merge. Merging
more eagerly lowers the number of parts at the cost of higher write
amplification, and the algorithms below strike that trade-off differently.

## Simple {#merge-selectors-simple}

The default. `Simple` slides a window over the parts of a partition and allows a
merge when the total size of the range is at least `merge_selector_base` times
the size of its largest part; the base is lowered as parts age or the partition
fills up.

`Simple` does not know about merge levels: the number of parts per level is
emergent rather than bounded, and the size-ratio check discourages merging a
small part into a much larger one until the parts are old enough or `OPTIMIZE`
is run.

## StochasticSimple {#merge-selectors-stochastic-simple}

A variant of `Simple` that slides the merge window stochastically, controlled by
`merge_selector_blurry_base_scale_factor`.

## Trivial {#merge-selectors-trivial}

Merges a fixed number of parts at a time. Mainly useful for testing.

## Manual {#merge-selectors-manual}

Disables background merge selection; merges are scheduled explicitly with
`SYSTEM MERGE` queries.

## FluidLSM {#merge-selectors-fluid-lsm}

`FluidLSM` implements the Fluid LSM-tree merge policy from the Dostoevsky paper:

> Niv Dayan, Stratos Idreos. "Dostoevsky: Better Space-Time Trade-Offs for
> LSM-Tree Based Key-Value Stores via Adaptive Removal of Superfluous Merging",
> SIGMOD'18. https://doi.org/10.1145/3183713.3196927

The paper observes that in an LSM-tree the I/O cost of a write is paid equally
at all levels through the merges it participates in, while the cost of lookups
and the space amplification derive mostly from the largest level. Merging at
all levels but the largest is therefore mostly "superfluous", and removing it
improves write cost without hurting read cost or space amplification.

In ClickHouse terms, parts are runs and `MergeTreePartInfo::level` - the number
of merges a part has survived - is the LSM-tree level. A merge of parts at level
`l` produces a part at level `l + 1`, so levels below the top play the role of
the smaller LSM-tree levels. `FluidLSM` keeps at most `K` parts
(`merge_selector_fluid_lsm_k`, default 9) at each level below the largest
occupied level and at most `Z` parts (`merge_selector_fluid_lsm_z`,
default 20) at the largest level, and schedules a merge when a level
overflows its bound:

- `K = 1` and `Z = 1` approximates leveling: every incoming part is merged into
  the existing parts of its level as soon as possible.
- Large `K` and large `Z` approximates tiering: parts accumulate and merge only
  when a level is full.
- `K > 1` and `Z = 1` is "Lazy Leveling": tiering at all levels but the
  largest, and leveling at the largest level.

The default `Z` is above 1: merging the largest parts is the most expensive
merging, and under a merge backlog an eagerly leveled top can starve the
background pool while fresh parts pile up. Keeping up to `Z` parts at the
top costs little on the read side - queries read all parts of a partition
regardless - and reduces merge work substantially. Set `Z = 1` for full lazy
leveling: the fewest parts and the least stale data, at the cost of the most
merge work.

Since a merge produces a level `l + 1` part rather than filling a
fixed-capacity level, the merge fanout `K + 1` plays the role of the size ratio
`T` of the paper, and levels grow organically towards the top.

The bounds decide *when* a merge is scheduled; `Simple` decides *what* is
merged: when a lower level overflows, the whole partition is delegated to the
`Simple` selector, so under a merge backlog it picks the same wide, possibly
cross-level windows it would pick itself, while under light load the
overflowing run is usually the only window that qualifies. If the scan finds
nothing - for example when the overflowing run is too small to pass the ratio
check - the same-level run is merged so a level can never be stuck over its
bound. An overflow of the largest level is merged directly as windows
covering `Z` + 1 largest-level parts, sweeping any lower-level parts between
them; in that case the partition is not delegated to `Simple` - its windows
could overlap the swept parts - but overflowing lower-level runs disjoint
from the sweep are still merged, so they cannot pile up behind a stalled
largest-level merge.

Compared to `Simple`:

- `FluidLSM` guarantees a worst-case bound of about `K * L + Z` parts per
  partition instead of an emergent part count.
- Lower levels merge only once they fill up, lowering write amplification.
- `Z` tunes how eagerly the largest level compacts, which is the merge work
  that actually bounds the number of parts and the amount of outdated data a
  query sees.

Analytical queries read all parts of a partition regardless of how the parts
were merged, so the lazier lower levels are close to free on the read side; the
expected benefit is lower write amplification and a tighter bound on the number
of parts. The selector is opt-in and experimental.

The parts of the Dostoevsky design that do not map to `MergeTree` are not
implemented: there is no per-level Bloom filter allocation (ClickHouse uses a
sparse primary index rather than per-run Bloom filters probed by point
lookups), and `K`/`Z` are static table settings rather than being tuned
automatically from the workload.
